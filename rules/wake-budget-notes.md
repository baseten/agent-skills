# Notes — wake-budget

Reasoning for `rules/wake-budget.md`. Deliberately *not* bundled into
each skill: a copy of this per consumer would be design history with no reader,
and the audience for it is a person editing the rule, who has this checkout.

Moved here with the rule itself, from `backlog-orchestrator`, *Arming the wait when nothing is in flight*, and the part of *API budget and read discipline* that bounded a deferring wake, with their notes (#144). "The run" in the moved entries is `backlog-orchestrator`'s, as it stood when the entry was written. The reply watch, the owner-queue report on a quiet wake and the attendance-gated settle trigger stayed in that skill: they are about its items and its settle sequence, not about how often a wake may fire.

**Why this is a shared rule (#144):** the wake is armed by whichever party owns supervision — `backlog-orchestrator` over a wave, and a PR supervisor running its own loop over one PR — and the budget must bind both identically, or the unbounded watcher it exists to stop comes back through whichever copy is looser.

## A subscription and a check-in, both

**Why "costs nothing between firings" had to go:** the earlier text asserted that a durable subscription and a scheduled wake are free, and nothing available here documents that. What can be relied on is weaker and enough: whatever a subscription costs is paid at arming rather than per check, so it does not scale with how long the run waits. Treating it as zero is how a run justifies arming several, and hedging it with polling of the run's own invention spends exactly what the subscription was armed to avoid — while the bounded check-in, which looks like the same hedge, is the backstop the section requires and must not be cut with it.

**Why a worker the run cannot hear from arms the check-in early (owner feedback on a 34-issue run, Oct 2026):** the section opened "while workers are running, their completions are the events", and on the remote-session tier that is false — a worker's report is pulled, never pushed, and a worker stopped on a permission prompt emits nothing. The run read blocked state every cycle as required, and flagged a stalled head at 30 minutes, but nothing scheduled a cycle while workers were in flight, so a worker sat blocked on a Bash prompt for three and a half hours until the owner read the session by hand. The detection rules were right; the cadence they assume did not exist. So the check-in is armed at dispatch on that tier, and it stays the run's one check-in rather than a second watch, which is what keeps protected rule 9 ("wakes are bounded") intact.

**Why 20 minutes held, not the doubling backoff:** the doubling exists because a quiet watch is usually waiting on a person, who may be a day away. An in-flight worker is a running cost, and a blocked one costs more every interval; `swarm` judges it on elapsed time (30 minutes unmoved reported, 2 hours `NEEDS_USER`), and a doubling cadence reaches 80 and 160 minutes between firings by its fourth wake, so a block would surface hours late again. Twenty minutes is the backoff's own first step, so no new number enters the rule, and it is inside the owner's 30-minute ceiling. It holds only while a worker is working: one already held for the owner is waiting on a person, which is the case the doubling was built for, and holding 20 minutes over it would spend the budget in under three hours on a permission prompt the owner had not yet seen — where the backoff gives them most of a day. The budget still binds: eight unproductive firings at 20 minutes is 160 minutes, past the point at which every worker that caused them is already `NEEDS_USER`, and its stop names them alive.

**Why a worker raised for an unmoved head stops counting as working (pre-merge audit of #199, round 1):** the first version held 20 minutes until every worker was held for the owner, and a stalled worker raised `NEEDS_USER` at two hours is not one of `swarm`'s holds. A zombie session reading working with an unmoved head therefore pinned the interval until the budget was spent at 160 minutes, ending the watch on PRs that were waiting on a day-away reviewer. Once raised it waits on a person exactly as a held worker does, so the backoff resumes at the step the count gives. The same round named the runtime *status* as the delta source, never an activity timestamp, which would make every firing productive and the count never rise; made adoption by a resumed run a trigger beside dispatch; and said re-arming replaces the pending check-in, since a cycle woken by a subscription that left the old one pending would keep its stale prompt, and one armed beside it would be a second chain.

**Why a quiet wake leaves the pending check-in alone (pre-merge audit of #199, round 2):** round 1's fix said a cycle woken by anything else re-arms the check-in the same way, and the re-audit found the hole it opened: subscription wakes that observed nothing, every thirty minutes, each replaced the pending check-in with a fresh one further out, so it never fired, the count never reached eight, and after a compaction a re-armed count of zero reset the budget outright. Base had no such path, because a subscription wake never touched the check-in. The uncancellable case then needed an end: the author's blind re-audit after round 2 found that a check-in which could not be disarmed at return would fire a prompt written before the return, run a full cycle and re-arm — against *The watch ends with the run* and a user stop. Every prompt therefore opens with a liveness guard that reads the run's latest checkpoint or stop record and ends without acting where the run has since returned or stopped; the wake stays reported as bound to the run, so a resume still asks rather than runs beside it. The same audit narrowed runtime status as a delta to the transitions a run acts on — into or out of blocked, or to returned on a worker not already held — since a held mismatch worker keeps its own wakes and can flip between working and returned with no progress, the activity-timestamp failure in a milder form. A worker held on a pending prompt cannot flip out of blocked on its own, since it gets no turn until someone answers. A second re-audit keyed the guard to the session that armed the prompt, so a predecessor's uncancelled check-in reading a resumed run's newer, live checkpoint ends rather than running a second orchestrator. Only a delta may move it — the same delta that resets the count, a dispatch or adoption aside (below) — and a runtime that cannot cancel a one-shot arms nothing beside it. The same round made a stall raise lapse when the worker's head advances again, the counterpart of *Blocked workers*' changed reading.

**Why a dispatch re-arms the check-in, and what an uncancellable one costs (final pre-merge audit of #199):** the in-flight cadence was promised "from dispatch". But a quiet wake re-arms only on a delta, so a reader could keep a check-in pending at the 4-hour step through a dispatch, and an uncancellable one blocked any re-arm until it fired. Either way the first 20-minute read could come up to four hours late, which is the blocked-prompt incident again. A dispatch or adoption is now itself a re-arm trigger, at the count its cycle leaves. It can only bring the firing forward, so it cannot postpone one the way the round-2 quiet wakes did. "This session's own" pending check-in, so a predecessor's surviving one never stands in for the resumed run's, since the guard would end it with workers in flight. Arming beside an uncancellable check-in was tried and withdrawn in the same pass. It needed a guard that ends a superseded firing by comparing it with a recorded current check-in. `swarm` invoked directly records none, so the superseded firing would run a full cycle there and leave two live chains. A firing-time tie could do the same anywhere. So on such a runtime the delay stays bounded by the pending firing time — one backoff step, or a later allowance floor — and is stated and reported (the pending firing time is the earliest a block can be seen) rather than closed. Pinned by backlog-orchestrator#95.

**Why a worker that keeps pushing keeps 20 minutes, uncapped (owner's ruling on #199's round-2 residual, Oct 2026):** the re-audit noted that a remote worker whose countermand failed and keeps pushing makes every firing productive, so the in-flight cadence holds for as long as it pushes, and offered a wall-clock cap on "working" as the fix. The owner declined it. A worker that pushes is progressing, and the cadence exists to see it stop, not to ration how long it may work; a cap would put a working worker on the doubling backoff and reopen the hours-late surfacing the in-flight check-in was armed to close. What a stuck worker does is stop advancing, and the 30-minute report and 2-hour `NEEDS_USER` cover that, after which the count binds. A PR receiving continual deltas has the same property on base, and no one bounds that either: the budget is a guard on waking to learn nothing, not on waking to watch work happen.

## The budget and the backoff

**Why every check-in is one-shot (same run):** its check-in prompts restated a plan from hours earlier. The rule already required each re-armed prompt to carry the latest state, but a recurring trigger is armed once and fires the same text every time — so it satisfies "the prompt carries the count" on its first firing and never again. One-shot, re-armed by each firing, is the only shape in which the requirement can hold, and it applies to a check-in armed in flight exactly as to one armed at settle.

**What two unbounded check-ins cost:** the previous rule — "re-arm each time it fires and finds nothing, stop when everything is merged or closed" — terminates only on an event that may never come. Two sessions running exactly that loop against already-merged PRs billed $33.45 and $59.60 doing nothing but waking hourly, reading no change, and re-arming. The budget-and-backoff shape (8 unproductive wakes — see the collapse note below for why unreadable wakes count too — 20 minutes doubling to a 4-hour cap, ~21 hours total) is sized so a watch survives a night and a working day waiting on a human reviewer but a forgotten one dies in single-digit dollars. (#50's own text estimated ~14 hours; the arithmetic was corrected in review — 20+40+80+160+240×4 = 1,260 minutes.)

**Why the counter lives in the wake's prompt:** the run that leaked one session was compacted twice mid-run. A counter held in session memory does not survive a compaction or the gap between firings, so it resets silently and the budget never binds. The re-armed prompt is the only storage that provably reaches the next firing.

**The four findings the seam produced, kept because they are the argument for collapsing it.** Clearing on *any* successful read failed when GraphQL was refused and REST healthy: every wake read REST, cleared the count, was refused on the GraphQL read it needed, and re-armed forever. Clearing on *the previously blocked resource* failed two further ways — where exhaustion alternates between buckets each wake reads the resource that blocked the last one, clears, and is refused by the other, so the count never passed 1 though every wake was throttled; and a secondary limit is tied to no resource at all, so a count it caused had nothing that could ever clear it and would have stopped a healthy watch. Then the trigger itself: the deferral rule fires on three conditions and each restatement of it recognized one, so a wake deferring preemptively on a low allowance — refused by nothing — incremented neither counter.

Every one of those is a clearing or trigger condition drifting from the rule it shadowed, which is the failure mode a second counter makes available and a single counter does not have. Under the collapsed design none of the four is expressible: there is one count, it rises on any unproductive wake, and only an observed delta clears it.

What deliberately does **not** collapse is the resource dimension: deferral stays per resource, because it decides which reads may be attempted, while the budget decides whether waking is worth doing at all. Those are different questions and conflating them produced the middle two defects above.

**Why this was two counters and is now one:** the split came from a true observation and the wrong conclusion. A throttled wake genuinely cannot claim "nothing changed" — it read nothing, so it does not know — so exempting it from the budget as it then was — no-ops only — was right. But the exemption removed the check-in's only termination guarantee, and rather than questioning the split, a second budget was bolted on beside the first.

That seam produced four review findings in a row, each a real unbounded-watcher path and each fixed narrowly enough to leave the next one open: clearing on any successful read, then on the previously blocked resource, then on a condition restated instead of referenced. The final design is sound but baroque, and every defect lived in the interaction between two counters rather than in either one.

The conflation underneath was between **how long to keep waking** and **when to wake next**. Only the second differs by cause. So the budget is now single — a wake is unproductive whether it read and found nothing or could not read at all, since both spend money to learn nothing — while the schedule stays per cause (the reset/`Retry-After` floor where the deferral supplied one, the doubling backoff otherwise — whichever is later; a deferral supplying neither bound sets no floor, per `rules/watch-and-read-notes.md`, *Why a floorless deferral is defined rather than left implicit*) and so does the report, because a watch that expired against a contended allowance and one that expired on a quiet PR call for different remedies.

One consequence is worth stating: a wake that could not read never clears the count, because clearing requires an observed delta and it observed nothing. That is what makes the single counter bind on a permanently contended credential, and it is the property the second counter was invented to supply.

## A watch kept past a settled result

**Why a separate, shorter schedule (#183, owner's ruling):** after settle the run waits on a human reviewer, not on CI. Twenty minutes is too eager for that, and eight wakes is longer than anyone waits for a review to be noticed. About 50 minutes, then every 4 hours, three times, covers a working afternoon and stops. It is still a bounded budget with the same unproductive-wake rules, so protected rule 9 holds.
